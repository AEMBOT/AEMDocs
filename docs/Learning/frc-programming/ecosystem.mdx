# Intro to the FRC Ecosystem

As an FRC programmer, there's a relatively small number of potential hardware and software targets your team will probably end up using.
This page will walk you through the ones you'll be interacting with as an AEMBOT programmer.

## Hardware
Obviously, to program a robot, you need to understand the physical things you're programming. Here are some of your most common devices:

### The RoboRIO / SystemCore

<img
  src={RoboRIO}
  height="300px"
  alt="The RoboRIO from National Instruments. Labelled are features such as the USB ports, an SD card slot, the ethernet port, and the reset and user buttons."
/>
<img
  src={SystemCore}
  height="300px"
  alt="The new SystemCore device from Limelight."
/>
The RoboRIO (pre-2027) or SystemCore (2027 and beyond) are the primary control
devices for the robot. They both run linux-based operating systems, and can have
Java; C++; Python; LabVIEW; or, in the case of SystemCore, Blockly code deployed
to them to be run on startup. We'll also usually plug a USB thumb stick into it to contain logs.

Generally speaking, your main method of connecting to the device will be via the radio (more on that below). If you're connected this way, you'll
be able to access your RIO/SystemCore at the IP address 10.64.43.2. You can SSH into the RIO via the following command:

<Tabs>
  <TabItem value="rio" label="RoboRIO">
    ```bash 
      ssh admin@10.64.43.2
    ```
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="systemcore" label="SystemCore">
    ```bash 
      ssh systemcore@10.64.43.2
    ```
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

Before each season (or sometimes during the season), you'll need to image your RoboRIO or SystemCore with the latest operating system. You can find
instructions for the RIO [here](https://docs.wpilib.org/en/2025/docs/zero-to-robot/step-3/roborio2-imaging.html), and instructions for SystemCore
[here](https://docs.wpilib.org/en/latest/docs/zero-to-robot/step-3/imaging-your-systemcore.html).

### Radio

<img src={VHRadio} width="200px" alt="A VividHosting 109 radio." />

The radio will be your primary method of communication with the robot. As of
2026, we use the VH-109 radio. There are four main ways to connect to this
radio, depending on how it's configured:

- **Via a direct ethernet connection to it.** This will be your only method of connection while at comps in the pit. 
- **Via the FMS (Field Management System).** This is the system FIRST uses to manage teams' robots and connect them to their driver stations. At a comp, you'll need to take your radio up to be configured for FMS by event staff. 
- **Via Wi-Fi (setting the radio up as an access point).** Your radio will essentially act as a wi-fi router. You can connect to your robot from your device's wi-fi interface. 
- **Via another radio.** This is the method that we use most often in the shop, as it's generally more reliable and faster than the above option due to its usage of Wi-Fi 6E. You'll connect your laptop via ethernet to a stationary radio, and it will communicate wirelessly with the robot's radio. The below diagram illustrates this setup.

<img src="https://docs.wpilib.org/en/latest/_images/VH-109-2-radios.drawio.svg" />

See [here](https://docs.wpilib.org/en/latest/docs/zero-to-robot/step-3/radio-programming.html) for a guide to flashing and configuring your radio.

### CTRE Hardware

Cross the Road Electronics (CTRE) is probably our most used vendor for electronics on our robots; they produce almost all of our sensors and motors.
Sometimes you may hear them called Phoenix devices, since we use the [Phoenix 6 libary](https://v6.docs.ctr-electronics.com/en/stable/docs/api-reference/api-usage/index.html) to control them.
All of CTREs devices can be configured and tested via the desktop application [Phoenix Tuner X](https://v6.docs.ctr-electronics.com/en/stable/docs/tuner/index.html) on Windows.

The majority of CTRE's devices (all of the ones we use) operate on a CAN bus. As opposed to directly individually wiring devices to the RIO/SystemCore, all
devices are interconnected by a bus; a shared pathway over which data is sent serially. Each device has a numerical CAN ID, and every message contains the ID that the message is originating from or targeting.
The RoboRIO has one integrated CAN bus using CAN 2.0, and the SystemCore has five integrated CAN-FD (faster) busses. CTRE also offers the CANivore to create a
separate CAN-FD bus, but this'll be disallowed in 2028. In older RIO-based bots, you'll often see a separate CANivore bus for the drivetrain.

The CTRE devices we use most are:

- **TalonFX**: This is the **motor controller** used for CTRE's Kraken and Falcon motors, and probably the CTRE device we interact with most.
  - [CTRE Hardware Reference](https://v6.docs.ctr-electronics.com/en/stable/docs/hardware-reference/talonfx/index.html)
  - [CTRE Software Docs](https://v6.docs.ctr-electronics.com/en/stable/docs/api-reference/device-specific/talonfx/index.html)
  - {/* TODO add AEMLib docs */}
- **CANcoder**: The CANcoder is, as the name indicates, an **absolute encoder** that can communicate via CAN. It senses the magnetic field of a magnet attached to
  your rotating mechanism, thereby determining its rotation. Unlike a **relative encoder**, it does not lose its state when it loses power. It's especially good
  for mechanisms that won't have a very precise starting config, and generally good for any mechanism where its position is important since it's resistant to
  unexpected power cuts or brown-outs.
  - [CTRE Hardware Reference](https://v6.docs.ctr-electronics.com/en/stable/docs/hardware-reference/cancoder/index.html)
  - {/* TODO add AEMLib docs */}
- **CANrange**: The CANrange is a **time-of-flight sensor**; it fires off a laser and measures how long it takes the light to bounce back to determine the distance
  to the surface it hits. If you're familiar with game dev or rendering, you can think of it like a raycast!
  - [CTRE Hardware Reference](https://v6.docs.ctr-electronics.com/en/stable/docs/hardware-reference/canrange/index.html)
  - {/* TODO add AEMLib docs */}
- **Pigeon 2**: The Pigeon 2 is an **IMU**; it provides us with the acceleration and rotation of the robot. We generally use it as our exclusive source of truth for
  robot yaw, since it's generally substantially more accurate than dead reckoning or vision.
  - [CTRE Hardware Reference](https://v6.docs.ctr-electronics.com/en/stable/docs/hardware-reference/pigeon2/index.html)

### Limelight Cameras

<img
  src={Limelight}
  width="400px"
  alt="The Limelight 4, a camera and coprocessor encased in a rectangular black box."
/>
We use Limelights for our **computer vision (CV)**. Our most common use for them
is to detect field-mounted
[AprilTags](https://docs.wpilib.org/en/stable/docs/software/vision-processing/apriltag/apriltag-intro.html)
(tags similar to QR codes, which each have a unique numerical ID assigned to
them), and use their positions relative to the camera to determine the robot's
field position.

:::note
  The systems that work together to determine the position of the robot on the field are collectively known as its **odometry** systems.
:::

Limelight offers the most plug-&-play experience currently available for FRC CV; each Limelight camera has an integrated
coprocessor, a second computer that performs pose-estimation on its own without straining the resources of the RIO / SystemCore.

Our team generally uses the naming convention `limelight-<mount-position>` (e.g. `limelight-left`, `limelight-turret`) for our hostnames, so you can
generally access the web dashboard over radio via `http://limelight-<mount-position>.local:5801`. If you don't know the hostname or it hasn't been set,
you can find the limelight via the [Limelight Hardware Manager](https://docs.limelightvision.io/docs/resources/downloads).

Limelight also produces color-vision cameras, which we may sometimes use to detect game pieces via CV.

### Other common hardware
Some other devices you'll commonly use include:
- **Beam breaks**: Beam breaks are similar to **time-of-flight** sensors in that they operate by shooting out a laser. They are different however in that rather than having the 
  laser reflect off a detected object, the laser travels directly into a seperate sensor. By detecting when the sensor does not recieve the laser, one can determine when there 
  is an object between the two components, thereby _breaking the beam_. If you look at a garage door, it very likely uses a beam break to detect when an object is obstructing
  its closing. 

## Software
Now for what you _actually_ care about as a programmer: software! These are the most common software tools you'll use:

### VSCode
<img src={VSCode} alt="A screenshot of the VSCode editor. On the left is a list of sidebar menus, with the file tree currently open. On the bottom is an integrated terminal. In the center is the actual code being written." />
VSCode is our primary **IDE** (Integrated Developer Environment, or in other words, fancy text editor). It's very flexible and easy to use once you get the hang 
of it. For FRC, you'll want to use the WPILib-specific version of VSCode. This will create a new install of VSCode on your device with different plugins, 
preferences, and overall state.

### WPILib
<WPILibLogo height="100px" width="100px" alt="The WPILib logo" />
WPILib is the primary **library** we use to program the RIO / SystemCore; it is what essentially everything we write is built on top of. It has bindings for C++,
Python, and Java (which is what we use). It offers tons of methods to interface with the robot's hardware, access FMS data, and generally just make FRC
programming significantly easier!

There are a multitude of libraries built on top of WPILib (called **Vendor Dependencies**), such as ChoreoLib for following preplanned trajectories in auto,
Phoenix 6 for controlling CTRE devices, and AdvantageKit for making logs significantly richer and easier to program.

### AdvantageScope
<img src={AScope} alt="AdvantageScope is open to a match log. Visible in the center is a 3D field with an accurate model of the 2026 robot Anubis. In three pop-out windows, there is a display of a video recording of the match, a replay of the drivers' controller inputs, and a line graph displaying the shooter's flywheel velocity in meters/second." />

Speaking of logging, AdvantageScope (or AScope) is the frontend counterpart to AdvantageKit (though it doesn't necessarily need AKit to operate).
Created by team [6328 Mechanical Advantage](https://github.com/Mechanical-Advantage), AdvantageScope offers almost everything you'd need from a log viewer
and programming dashboard. 

It accepts data from a log file, most commonly in the `.wpilog` format, or from NetworkTables, meaning it can be used to view live 
data on a connected robot. It then provides a multitude of ways to visualize this data, such as observing the raw values at a timestamp,
plotting them on a line graph, displaying poses and mechanism positions on a field, or visualizing the state of a swerve drive.

### Choreo 
<img src={Choreo} alt="Choreo is open to an autonomous trajectory from 2026, currently editing one of the waypoints along the path. The full path involves driving to the center of the field, picking up gamepieces, and then driving back to score them." />

Choreo is an application to create highly optimized drive trajectories to follow in autonomous (and in tele-op in some cases). It also allows you to
specify commands to be run at points on that trajectory, thereby enabling you to plot an entire autonomous routine in Choreo. The companion vendor dep
ChoreoLib allows the robot to read and carry out these trajectories and routines.

### The DriverStation
<img src={DSLegacy} alt="The legacy Driver Station. It is on the main tab, displaying buttons to enable or disable the robot and switch the mode, indicators to display the status of communications, code, and joysticks, and a console displaying warnings." /> 
<img src={DS} alt="The new alpha Driver Station on its main page. It displays much of the same information as the legacy driverstation, but its design is significantly more sleek and rounded, utilizing more iconography as opposed to textual labels." />

The FRC DriverStation is, as the name would imply, the software drivers utilize to drive the robot. 
We will usually have a designated laptop (also called the "driver station", confusingly) for the purposes of running the DS (software) in matches, and a second
similar laptop for running the DriverStation and VSCode in the pit (called the "pit laptop").

### Elastic
<img src={Elastic} alt="The Elastic dashboard opened to the 'Teleoperated' tab. In a grid layout it displays information such as the robot's position and auto trajectory on the field, the amount of time left in the match, and the positions of all the swerve drive modules. In addition is a dropdown to select the desired auto routine to run." />

Elastic is our preferred **driver dashboard**. It pulls data from NetworkTables (more on that in a later chapter) to display any desired info to the drivers. 
It also allows us to set up options for the drivers to input before a match, such as the auto routine we want to perform. Generally, during a match it'll 
be part of the operator's job to look at Elastic and inform the driver about the technical details of the robot (ie. current draw if they need to drive 
less aggressively, odometry accuracy if they need to re-zero).

It's extremely easily to configure; one simply needs to drag a widget for any NetworkTables value and
